Becoming a Canadian Citizen After Permanent Residence

Citizenship Grant

A citizenship grant is the process by which a permanent resident of Canada becomes a Canadian citizen by application — what is commonly called naturalization. IRCC "grants" citizenship under the Citizenship Act to permanent residents who meet the residence, tax-filing, language and other requirements.

This is the most common pathway to Canadian citizenship. It is different from a citizenship certificate, which is proof for people who are already citizens — the two are compared in detail below.

What Does "Citizenship Grant" Mean?

It means you apply to officially become a Canadian citizen after meeting the required conditions while living in Canada as a permanent resident.

Once approved, you:

  • Take the Oath of Citizenship
  • Receive your citizenship certificate
  • Become a full Canadian citizen

Who Is Eligible?

To qualify for citizenship through naturalization (citizenship grant), you generally must:

1 Be a Permanent Resident

You must hold valid PR status and not be under a removal order.

2 Meet Physical Presence Requirements

You must have been physically present in Canada for at least 1,095 days (3 years) within the last 5 years before applying.

It's important to track your travel history carefully. Even small miscalculations can delay your application.

Note: Days spent in Canada as a temporary resident or protected person before becoming a permanent resident count as half-days (0.5), up to a maximum of 365 credited days. This means some applicants may qualify sooner than they expect.

3 File Your Taxes (If Required)

You must have filed taxes for at least 3 years within the 5-year period if required under Canadian tax law.

4 Language Requirements

If you are between 18 and 54 years old, you must show adequate knowledge of English or French (CLB 4 or higher).

5 Pass the Citizenship Test

Applicants between 18 and 54 must pass a citizenship test about:

  • Canadian history
  • Government and political system
  • Rights and responsibilities
  • Canadian values and symbols

6 No Criminal or Security Prohibitions

Must not be prohibited on criminal or any security grounds under the Citizenship Act.

Why Apply for Citizenship?

Many permanent residents ask: "Why should I apply if I already have PR?"

Here's why citizenship matters:

  • You receive a Canadian passport
  • You can vote in federal and provincial elections
  • You can run for political office
  • You cannot lose status due to residency obligations
  • You have full protection as a Canadian citizen

Permanent residence requires maintaining residency days. Citizenship removes that concern. It gives stability for life.

From Application to Oath

The Application Process, Stage by Stage

1 Confirm Eligibility and Calculate Your Physical Presence

Before anything else, calculate your days in Canada using IRCC's official physical presence calculator. Every trip outside Canada — including same-day border runs and short US visits — must be accounted for.

What applicants commonly get wrong: forgetting short trips, relying on memory instead of passport stamps and travel records, and applying with exactly 1,095 days. Small discrepancies between your declared travel history and border records are a leading cause of delays and requests for additional documents. Apply with a comfortable buffer of extra days.

2 Prepare and Submit the Application

Gather your documents: the application form, photos, copies of your PR document and identification, proof of language ability (if you are 18–54), and your travel history. Pay the government fee and submit online or on paper.

What applicants commonly get wrong: unsigned forms, missing language evidence, and confusion about PR cards. An expired PR card does not disqualify you — what matters is that you hold valid PR status and are not under a removal order or unfulfilled conditions.

Government fees change periodically. For current amounts, see the official IRCC fee list.

3 Acknowledgement and Processing

IRCC confirms receipt of a complete application (an "acknowledgement of receipt"), then begins verification: physical presence, immigration history, and criminal and security background checks. You may be asked for additional documents — respond by the deadline given, or the application can be returned or refused.

Processing times change with IRCC's workload. For the current figure, check the official IRCC processing times tool.

4 Citizenship Test and Interview

Applicants aged 18–54 are invited to take the citizenship test: 20 multiple-choice and true/false questions, 30 minutes, with 15 correct answers needed to pass. It is based on the official study guide, Discover Canada.

If you do not pass the first attempt, IRCC schedules a retake. If you do not pass the second, you attend a hearing with a citizenship official rather than being refused automatically.

What applicants commonly get wrong: underestimating the test and not studying the guide, and missing the interview component — an official may verify your original documents, review your travel history, and assess your English or French during the interaction.

5 Decision and the Oath of Citizenship

Once approved, you are invited to a citizenship ceremony. You become a Canadian citizen at the moment you take the Oath of Citizenship — and you receive your citizenship certificate afterwards as proof. Adults and children 14 and over must attend and take the oath.

Common Reasons Applications Are Refused or Returned

  • Physical presence shortfall — declared days don't meet 1,095, or travel records contradict the declared history
  • Incomplete applications — missing signatures, documents, or fees result in the package being returned unprocessed
  • Language or test requirements not met — for applicants aged 18–54
  • Prohibitions — certain criminal charges, convictions, or security issues under the Citizenship Act
  • Misrepresentation — providing false information can lead to refusal and a five-year bar from applying again

Most refusals are avoidable. The majority trace back to presence miscalculations and documentation gaps — exactly the areas where a professional review before submission pays for itself.

Two Different Applications for Two Different Situations

Citizenship Grant vs. Citizenship Certificate

These two terms are often confused because both end with a citizenship certificate in your hands. The difference is what the application does: a grant makes you a citizen; a certificate proves you already are one.

Citizenship GrantCitizenship Certificate (Proof of Citizenship)
What it isThe process of becoming a Canadian citizen by application (naturalization)An official document proving you are already a Canadian citizen
Who needs itPermanent residents who meet the eligibility requirementsPeople who are already citizens but have no proof — most often those born outside Canada to a Canadian parent
When you applyAfter meeting the physical presence, tax-filing and other requirements as a PRAny time you need to confirm or document existing citizenship
What you receiveCitizenship itself — you take the Oath at a ceremony and then receive a certificateThe certificate document only; your legal status does not change
Typical scenarioA permanent resident who has lived in Canada for 3 of the last 5 yearsA person born abroad to a Canadian parent applying for proof of citizenship by descent, or someone replacing a lost certificate

If you were born outside Canada and have a Canadian parent (or, under Bill C-3, a Canadian grandparent or earlier ancestor), you may already be a citizen — in that case you do not apply for a grant at all. Read our guide to the citizenship certificate and citizenship by descent.
